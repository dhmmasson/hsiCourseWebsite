Course Project: Distributed Control and Command Center (CCC)

Project brief for the design of a distributed Control and Command Center for a nano-satellite.

Course Project: Distributed Control and Command Center (CCC)

Project Overview

The client wants to operate a nanosatellite as part of an educational and scientific program, while ensuring a professional level of reliability, traceability, and long-term sustainability.

Beyond simply flying a satellite, the ambition is to:

  • Train students to real space operations,
  • Enable scientific missions led by different payload teams,
  • Ensure continuity of operations over the full mission lifetime,
  • Preserve all operational and scientific knowledge generated by the mission.

To achieve this, the client expresses the need for a complete ground segment, organized around three complementary and cooperating subsystems:

  • A Command Center to operate the satellite,
  • One or several Mission Centers to manage payloads and scientific exploitation,
  • A Ground Station to physically communicate with the satellite.

The client insists that this ground segment must be distributed across multiple schools, accessible to both staff and students, and robust enough to operate for years.

Problem Statement

Command Center

The Command Center is the place from which the satellite is actually flown. It represents the operational brain of the mission, where decisions are taken, actions are coordinated, and the overall behavior of the spacecraft is monitored over time. From the point of view of the organization operating the satellite, the Command Center is responsible for ensuring that the satellite remains functional, safe, and useful throughout its entire lifetime.

Inside the Command Center, operators prepare the activities that will be carried out by the satellite. These activities are not improvised: they are defined through procedures that describe, step by step, how the satellite should be commanded in a given situation. Before being used in real operations, these procedures are tested in a simulated environment that reproduces the behavior of the satellite as faithfully as possible. This allows operators and students to rehearse operations, validate their understanding, and detect potential issues without risking the spacecraft.

Once operations are executed, everything that happens is carefully recorded. Telemetry received from the satellite, commands sent to it, operational decisions, and configuration changes are stored and preserved. This long-term memory of the mission is essential both for technical reasons, such as anomaly investigation, and for educational purposes, since it allows future teams to learn from past operations. To protect this knowledge, the data is duplicated and secured so that a technical failure does not result in an irreversible loss of mission history.

The Command Center is also where the overall health of the satellite is assessed. Operators monitor its subsystems, follow the evolution of its orbit, and manage aspects related to flight dynamics. When maintenance actions are required, or when unexpected behavior occurs, the Command Center acts as the focal point for analysis and coordination. It concentrates the operational expertise of the satellite and ensures coherence between all ongoing activities.

In the NAASC context, the Command Center is not limited to a single physical location. Several schools may host rooms that replicate the Command Center environment. These rooms can be used either for real operations, for training, or remain inactive depending on the situation. This distributed approach allows students from different institutions to participate in realistic space operations while sharing the same tools, data, and documentation. Accessibility is therefore a key aspect: the Command Center must be usable by both experienced staff and students, while still preserving operational discipline and safety.

Mission Center

While the Command Center focuses on the satellite as a whole, the Mission Center is centered on what the satellite is meant to accomplish. Each Mission Center is associated with a specific payload or scientific objective, and its role is to transform raw satellite data into meaningful results.

Teams working in a Mission Center are typically composed of experts, scientists, or advanced students who deeply understand the payload they are responsible for. Their work starts well before data is received, by defining how the payload should be used, under which conditions, and for which scientific goals. Based on this preparation, they propose operational plans that describe what the payload should do and when. These plans are then transmitted to the Command Center, which integrates them into the global satellite operations.

Once data is collected, the Mission Center takes charge of processing and analyzing it. This often involves complex data processing chains, calibration steps, and the management of evolving parameters that change over the course of the mission. Maintaining consistency over time is crucial, as scientific results depend on knowing exactly how the data was produced and under which conditions.

The Mission Center also plays an important role in monitoring the health of the payload. Regular assessments are performed, and reports are produced to track performance and detect degradations. When anomalies occur, the Mission Center contributes its expertise to diagnose issues and propose corrective actions, in coordination with the Command Center.

Beyond internal use, the Mission Center is responsible for sharing results. This includes distributing data to authorized users, producing summaries of scientific outcomes, and contributing to public communication and educational outreach. At the same time, access to sensitive tools and data remains controlled, as the integrity of scientific processing must be preserved.

Like the Command Center, the Mission Center is expected to remain active for as long as the satellite exists, even if operations slow down over time. It represents the continuity of the scientific mission, ensuring that knowledge, data, and expertise are not lost.

Ground Station

The Ground Station is the physical interface between the satellite in orbit and the teams on Earth. It is through the Ground Station that signals are exchanged, making it an indispensable element of the ground segment.

During each pass of the satellite over the station, the Ground Station receives telemetry data that describes the state of the spacecraft and its payloads. At the same time, it transmits commands prepared by the Command Center, allowing operators to control the satellite remotely. These exchanges only occur during limited visibility windows, which makes their preparation and execution particularly important.

In addition to communications, the Ground Station contributes to knowing where the satellite is and how it moves. By measuring signals and timing information, it provides data that can be used to reconstruct the satellite’s orbit and support flight dynamics activities. This information feeds back into mission planning and helps anticipate future passes and operations.

Accurate time and frequency references are essential for these tasks. The Ground Station must therefore maintain synchronization with universal time and ensure that its equipment operates coherently. Stability and precision are key, as even small discrepancies can affect communication quality or orbital calculations.

Because it is exposed to the terrestrial environment, the Ground Station must be designed to withstand weather conditions, remain reliable over long periods, and be maintainable directly on site. It must also comply with local regulations and operate autonomously from an energy point of view when necessary. From the client’s perspective, the Ground Station is expected to be a robust, long-term infrastructure rather than a temporary or fragile installation.

Key HSI Challenges

  1. Knowledge continuity under high turnover
  2. Support for shift transitions and training
  3. Distributed control and governance
